# United States: The Endangered Species Act of 1973 — Summary

**Document type:** Legislation (official summary — original text)
**Jurisdiction:** United States (federal law)
**Official source:** https://www.fws.gov/law/endangered-species-act
**Statute citation:** 16 U.S.C. § 1531 et seq., Public Law 93-205

> This is an original, independently written summary of a real US federal statute. It is
> for educational orientation only and is not a substitute for the statute itself or
> professional legal advice.

## Overview

The Endangered Species Act (ESA) was signed into law on 28 December 1973 with broad
bipartisan support. It is widely regarded as one of the most powerful wildlife protection
laws in the world. Its stated purposes are to conserve endangered and threatened species
and the ecosystems on which they depend. As of recent official figures, the ESA covers
roughly 1,600–1,700 domestic species. Two federal agencies jointly implement the Act: the
**US Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS)**, responsible for terrestrial and freshwater species,
and the **National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS/NOAA Fisheries)**, responsible for
marine and anadromous species.

## Listing Process

A species may be proposed for listing as "endangered" (in danger of extinction throughout
all or a significant portion of its range) or "threatened" (likely to become endangered in
the foreseeable future) either by petition from the public or by the Services' own
initiative. Listing decisions must be based **solely on the best available scientific and
commercial data** — by law, potential economic impact cannot be considered at the listing
stage, though it can be considered later in critical habitat designation decisions. The
process typically includes a status review, a proposed rule published in the Federal
Register, a public comment period, and a final listing determination.

## Section 9: The "Take" Prohibition

Once a species is listed as endangered, Section 9 of the Act (codified at 16 U.S.C. § 1538)
makes it unlawful for any person to **"take"** that species within the United States or its
territorial waters. "Take" is defined broadly to include to harass, harm, pursue, hunt,
shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect, or to attempt any of these. Regulatory
interpretation has extended "harm" to include significant habitat modification or
degradation that actually kills or injures wildlife by significantly impairing essential
behaviors such as breeding, feeding, or sheltering. The take prohibition applies
automatically to endangered species and, for threatened species, only to the extent the
Services specify by a species-specific "4(d) rule." The Act also generally prohibits
importing, exporting, and interstate or foreign commercial trade of listed species.

## Section 7: Federal Agency Consultation

Section 7 requires every federal agency to ensure that any action it authorizes, funds, or
carries out is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of a listed species or
destroy/adversely modify its designated critical habitat. Where an action "may affect" a
listed species, the acting agency must formally consult with FWS or NMFS, which then issues
a "biological opinion." Purely private activity with no federal funding, permitting, or
authorization generally does not trigger Section 7 consultation, though it may still be
subject to the Section 9 take prohibition directly.

## Critical Habitat

The Services are generally required to designate "critical habitat" for listed species —
specific geographic areas containing features essential to conservation. A critical
habitat designation does not create a wildlife refuge, does not bar all private activity,
and does not by itself prohibit landowners from using their land; its main legal effect is
to trigger the Section 7 consultation requirement for actions with a federal nexus.

## Penalties and Enforcement

Violations of the ESA can result in civil and criminal penalties, including significant
fines and, for knowing violations, imprisonment. The Act includes a "citizen suit"
provision that allows private individuals and organizations to sue to enforce its
requirements, including against federal agencies.
