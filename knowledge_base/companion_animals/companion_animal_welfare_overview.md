# Companion Animal Welfare: General Principles — Overview

**Document type:** Educational material (original text)
**Jurisdiction:** Not jurisdiction-specific (general principles; consult local law for
enforceable rules)

> This is an original educational overview of widely accepted companion animal welfare
> principles. It does not state specific legal requirements for any jurisdiction. For legal
> questions about companion animal treatment (e.g. tethering/chaining rules, licensing,
> mandatory microchipping), consult `legislation/` documents for the relevant country, or
> note that this knowledge base may not yet cover that specific jurisdiction.

## Scope of "Companion Animals"

Companion animals (commonly called "pets") are animals kept primarily for companionship
rather than for agricultural production, research, or commercial exhibition. Dogs and cats
are the most common companion animals in most countries, but the category also includes
animals such as rabbits, small rodents, birds, reptiles, and aquarium fish kept in
households.

## Widely Recognised Welfare Needs

Drawing on general welfare science frameworks such as the Five Freedoms (see
`animal_welfare/five_freedoms_fawc_summary.md`), companion animal welfare guidance
commonly identifies the following needs:

- **Appropriate environment** — safe, suitably sized living space appropriate to the
  species, with protection from extreme temperatures and hazards.
- **Appropriate diet** — species-specific nutrition and access to fresh water.
- **Ability to exhibit normal behaviour** — opportunities for exercise, mental
  stimulation, and species-typical activity (e.g. scratching for cats, adequate exercise
  for dogs).
- **Appropriate housing with, or apart from, other animals** — social animals generally
  need companionship (their own species and/or humans), while some individuals or species
  need to be housed separately to avoid conflict or stress.
- **Protection from pain, injury, and disease** — including access to veterinary care.

## Continuous Confinement and Tethering: A Commonly Regulated Area

Long-term or permanent chaining/tethering of dogs, and similarly restrictive continuous
confinement of other companion animals, is a topic that receives significant attention in
companion animal welfare guidance internationally, because prolonged tethering can prevent
an animal from exhibiting normal behaviour, taking adequate exercise, and can contribute to
distress. However, **whether continuous tethering is illegal, restricted, or unregulated
varies substantially by country, and often by state/province or even municipality within a
country.** Some jurisdictions have specific statutory limits on tethering duration or
required tether length/type; others regulate it only indirectly through a general duty-of-
care or anti-cruelty provision (where a prosecutor or court would need to show the specific
practice caused unnecessary suffering); and others have no specific rule at all. This
knowledge base does not contain a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction survey of tethering laws at
this time — if asked about a specific jurisdiction not otherwise covered, the assistant
should say so rather than assume.

## Shelters, Rescue, and Population Management

Companion animal welfare organizations and government programs commonly address
overpopulation and shelter capacity through initiatives such as spay/neuter programs,
adoption promotion, and, in some jurisdictions, mandatory registration or microchipping of
dogs and cats. The specific legal requirements (if any) again vary significantly by
jurisdiction.
