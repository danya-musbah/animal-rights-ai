## What "Sentience" Means

In animal welfare science and policy, "sentience" generally refers to the capacity of an
animal to have subjective experiences — including the capacity to feel pain, distress,
pleasure, and other affective states — rather than the (much stronger and more contested)
philosophical claim of full self-awareness or human-like consciousness. Sentience is the
key concept underlying why animal welfare is treated as a matter of moral and, in many
jurisdictions, legal concern: an entity that can suffer has an interest in not suffering.

## The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012)

In July 2012, a group of prominent neuroscientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on
Consciousness at a conference at the University of Cambridge, publicly declaring that the
weight of neurobiological evidence indicates that non-human animals — including all
mammals, birds, and many other creatures such as octopuses — possess the neurological
substrates that generate consciousness, and that humans are not unique in this regard.
This declaration is widely cited in animal welfare science and policy discussions as a
significant, if informal, marker of expert scientific consensus on the plausibility of
sentience across a wide range of species.

## Widening Scientific Recognition

Over the past two decades, sentience research has expanded well beyond mammals and birds.
Areas of active study include:

- **Fish sentience** — evidence of nociception (pain-detection systems) and behavioural
  responses consistent with pain experience in several fish species has increasingly been
  cited by welfare scientists and policymakers reconsidering fish welfare in aquaculture
  and fishing.
- **Cephalopod sentience** — octopuses, squid, and cuttlefish show complex problem-solving
  behaviour, and the scientific and regulatory view of their capacity for pain has shifted
  substantially, as reflected in EU Directive 2010/63/EU's explicit inclusion of cephalopods
  in animal research protections (see
  `scientific_research/eu_directive_2010_63_animal_testing_summary.md`).
- **Decapod crustacean sentience** — the welfare status of crabs, lobsters, and similar
  species has become an active policy question in several jurisdictions, following
  scientific reviews suggesting a credible capacity for pain perception.

## Sentience in Law and Policy

Some jurisdictions have moved to explicitly recognise animal sentience in law. For example,
the Lisbon Treaty (which entered into force in the EU on 1 December 2009) recognised
animals as sentient beings for the purposes of EU policy, requiring EU institutions and
Member States to pay full regard to animal welfare requirements when formulating and
implementing relevant policies. Other jurisdictions have passed dedicated "sentience"
statutes; the specific legal effect of such recognition (a general policy duty vs. a
directly enforceable right) varies significantly and should always be checked against the
specific statute in question rather than assumed.

## Distinguishing Science From Ethics

Establishing that an animal is sentient is a scientific/empirical question. What follows
from that finding — how much moral weight to give an animal's interests, and what legal
protections should result — is a separate ethical and political question on which
reasonable people, philosophical traditions, and legal systems differ (see
`animal_welfare/` and `organizations/` documents discussing animal rights vs. animal
welfare perspectives). This knowledge base distinguishes scientific findings about
sentience from ethical arguments built upon them, and does not present one particular
ethical conclusion as scientifically mandated.
