# European Union: Directive 2010/63/EU on the Protection of Animals Used for Scientific Purposes — Summary

**Document type:** Legislation / Regulation (official summary — original text)
**Jurisdiction:** European Union (transposed into national law by each Member State)
**Official source:** https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2010/63/oj

> This is an original, independently written summary of a real EU directive. It is for

> national transposing legislation (e.g. the UK's Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986,

## Overview

Directive 2010/63/EU governs the use of live animals for scientific and educational
purposes across the EU. It repealed the earlier Directive 86/609/EEC and became formally
applicable on 1 January 2013. It is widely regarded as one of the most comprehensive
animal-research welfare frameworks in the world, and is based on the Council of Europe's
earlier European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals used for Experimental
and other Scientific Purposes.

## Scope

The directive protects live non-human vertebrate animals, including independently feeding
larval forms and foetal forms of mammals from the last third of their normal development,
and — notably — **live cephalopods** (e.g. octopuses, squid), reflecting growing scientific
recognition of their capacity to experience pain and distress despite being invertebrates.

## The Three Rs Principle

The directive requires that any use of animals for scientific purposes apply the **Three
Rs**, a framework first articulated by W.M.S. Russell and R.L. Burch in 1959:

- **Replacement** — using non-animal methods wherever scientifically possible instead of
  live animals;
- **Reduction** — using the minimum number of animals necessary to obtain valid, reliable
  results; and
- **Refinement** — modifying procedures to minimise pain, suffering, distress, and lasting
  harm, and to improve animal welfare.

Every research project using animals must be formally evaluated for compliance with the
Three Rs and undergo a harm-benefit analysis before authorisation.

## Severity Classification

Procedures must be classified by the actual or expected level of suffering into one of
four severity categories: **non-recovery**, **mild**, **moderate**, and **severe**. This
classification feeds into ethical review, statistical reporting to the European Commission,
and constraints on how many times, and under what conditions, an individual animal may be
reused in further procedures.

## Special Protections

The directive imposes particular restrictions on the use of non-human primates (permitted
only where no alternative exists and subject to stricter justification), and prohibits the
use of great apes for scientific procedures altogether, with only extremely narrow,
exceptional derogations tied to serious threats to human health or the survival of the
species itself, subject to EU-level authorisation. As of current data compiled in
secondary sources, experimentation on great apes is banned in the EU, the UK, and a number
of other countries worldwide.

## Institutional Requirements

Member States must ensure that establishments breeding, supplying, or using animals for
scientific purposes are authorised and inspected, that project proposals are reviewed by a
competent authority, and that each establishment has an animal-welfare body responsible for
advising staff on Three Rs implementation and monitoring animal wellbeing.
