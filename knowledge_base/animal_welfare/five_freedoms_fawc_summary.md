# The "Five Freedoms" Animal Welfare Framework — Summary

**Document type:** Organizational policy / educational material (official summary — original text)
**Origin:** United Kingdom — Farm Animal Welfare Council (FAWC), building on the 1965 Brambell Report
**Jurisdiction:** International (widely adopted framework, not itself binding law)

> This is an original, independently written summary of a well-documented, internationally
> recognised animal welfare framework. It is educational, not a specific piece of
> legislation, and is not a substitute for the applicable law of any given jurisdiction.

## Origin

The "Five Freedoms" trace back to the 1965 UK government-commissioned Brambell Report on
the welfare of intensively farmed animals, which first articulated that farm animals should
at minimum be able to "stand up, lie down, turn around, groom themselves, and stretch their
limbs." The UK's Farm Animal Welfare Council (FAWC), an independent advisory body, later
formalised this thinking into the Five Freedoms framework, which has since become one of
the most widely cited conceptual foundations for animal welfare standards, guidance, and
legislation worldwide — including underpinning the welfare needs listed in the UK Animal
Welfare Act 2006 (see `legislation/uk_animal_welfare_act_2006_summary.md`).

## The Five Freedoms

1. **Freedom from hunger and thirst** — by ready access to fresh water and a diet to
   maintain full health and vigour.
2. **Freedom from discomfort** — by providing an appropriate environment, including
   shelter and a comfortable resting area.
3. **Freedom from pain, injury, and disease** — by prevention, or rapid diagnosis and
   treatment.
4. **Freedom to express normal behaviour** — by providing sufficient space, proper
   facilities, and the company of the animal's own kind.
5. **Freedom from fear and distress** — by ensuring conditions and treatment that avoid
   mental suffering.

## Influence and Evolution

The Five Freedoms have shaped animal welfare standards issued by governments, the World
Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH/OIE), and numerous welfare and veterinary
organisations. In recent decades, animal welfare science has increasingly moved toward a
complementary "Five Domains" model (nutrition, environment, health, behaviour, and mental
state), which some researchers argue offers a more dynamic, positive-welfare-oriented
framework than the original, more constraint-focused Five Freedoms. Both frameworks are
widely taught and referenced; the Five Freedoms remain the more commonly cited framework
in legislation and public communication due to their simplicity.

## Important Distinction: Framework vs. Law

The Five Freedoms are a **conceptual welfare science framework**, not a legally binding
instrument in themselves. They influence law (for example, underpinning the welfare needs
listed in section 9 of the UK Animal Welfare Act 2006) but are not directly enforceable as
"law" in most jurisdictions unless a specific statute or regulation incorporates them.
Users asking "is violating the Five Freedoms illegal" should be told that the answer
depends entirely on whether, and how, a specific jurisdiction's law has incorporated
these welfare needs into enforceable legal duties.
