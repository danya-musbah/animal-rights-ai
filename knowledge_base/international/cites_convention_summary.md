# CITES: The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora — Summary

## Overview

CITES is a multilateral treaty, opened for signature in Washington, D.C. in 1973 and in
force since 1975, that aims to ensure international trade in specimens of wild animals and
plants does not threaten their survival. It does not itself ban trade outright; rather, it
creates a licensing system that regulates trade based on how threatened a species is.

## The Appendix System

CITES organises protected species into three appendices, with different trade restrictions:

- **Appendix I** — species threatened with extinction. Commercial international trade in
  wild-caught specimens is generally prohibited, with only very limited exceptions (such as
  scientific research), typically requiring both export and import permits.
- **Appendix II** — species not necessarily currently threatened with extinction, but for
  which trade must be controlled to avoid becoming so. Trade is permitted with an export
  permit certifying the trade will not be detrimental to the species' survival.
- **Appendix III** — species that a particular country has identified as being subject to
  regulation within its own jurisdiction, and for which it has asked other CITES Parties
  for cooperation in controlling trade.

## Governance

CITES is administered through a Secretariat and a Conference of the Parties (CoP), which
meets periodically to review and amend the appendices, adopt resolutions, and address
implementation and enforcement issues. Decisions to move a species between appendices
require a vote of the Parties at a CoP, based on scientific and trade data submitted by
member states and expert bodies.

## Implementation and Limits

CITES itself is not directly enforceable; each of its many member states ("Parties") must
adopt national legislation to implement its provisions domestically, and enforcement
capacity varies significantly between countries. This has made illegal wildlife
trafficking — including poaching for ivory, rhino horn, and exotic pet trade — a persistent
enforcement challenge despite the treaty's broad international membership. CITES also
covers only trade; it does not directly regulate habitat destruction, domestic (non-trade)
hunting, or captive treatment of listed species, which fall to other international
instruments (e.g. the Convention on Biological Diversity) and national law.
